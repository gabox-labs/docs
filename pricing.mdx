Every pool has one ticket price, recomputed from the positions inside it:

```
ticket = EV x (1 + surcharge)
EV     = N / sum(1 / B_i)
```

`EV` is the harmonic mean of all backings, which is exactly the draw-weighted expected value of one pull: each item's value times its probability of being the one drawn.

## Why the harmonic mean

Draw weight is `1 / B`, so cheap items dominate the odds. The harmonic mean is dominated by the same cheap items, so price and odds always agree.

The wrong way to price a pool is the arithmetic average of values. One $8,000 grail in a pool of $10 commons would blow the average up to something nobody would pay, even though a buyer almost never draws the grail. The harmonic mean gets this right: **a few expensive grails barely move the ticket**, because you almost never draw them.

| Pricing rule | One grail added to a commons pool | Correct? |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic mean of values | Ticket explodes | No: buyers overpay for odds they do not have |
| Harmonic mean of backings | Ticket barely moves | Yes: price tracks what a pull is actually worth |

## Worked example

A tokenized-assets pool of cards holding 286 positions:

| Tier | Count | Backing each | Draw odds per pull |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common | 200 | $8 | ~92% combined |
| Uncommon | 60 | $30 | ~6.5% combined |
| Rare | 20 | $120 | ~0.5% combined |
| Epic | 5 | $600 | ~1 in 4,000 each |
| Grail | 1 | $8,000 | **~1 in 217,000** |

The math:

```
EV     = 286 / (200/8 + 60/30 + 20/120 + 5/600 + 1/8000)
       = $10.52
ticket = $10.52 x 1.15 = $12.10
```

A roughly $12 ticket buys a near-certain small card and a real, verifiable shot at the $8,000 grail. The odds table above is exactly what the pool page shows buyers, including the grail's 1-in-N number, plainly.

## What the buyer pays

At draw time the buyer is quoted:

- the current pool ticket (EV plus the 15% surcharge), plus
- the VRF service fee, with
- optional slippage bounds, since deposits and draws move EV between quote and settlement.

## No oracle anywhere

Notice what the formula does not contain: floor prices, external feeds, or appraisals. Every input is a backing that a depositor chose and collateralized. Pricing cannot be manipulated through an oracle because there is none; the only way to move the ticket is to deposit real, capital-backed positions.
